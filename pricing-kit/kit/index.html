The Retainer Pricing Kit / Josh Pearson

Same camera.
Same skill.
£1k or £10k.

I've sold social retainers for £1,000 a month and social retainers for £10,000 a month. I've done full-service retainers at about £15,000 a month. The camera didn't change. What changed was who I was selling to, what I was promising, what it cost me to make, and what I decided I wanted to earn. This kit is that thinking, written down, with a calculator to run your own numbers.

And to be straight with you: this isn't about everyone charging £10k a month. You can make very good money on £1,500 retainers. What matters is what each one pays you for the time it takes, and how many you can run without burning out. It all comes down to one thing: time in, money out. Know your numbers, then pick your route.

00 / My numbers / Time in, money out

My £1k retainers pay me more per hour than my £10k one did.

  • £1k£1,000 a month, three years running. 6 to 7 hours a month. About £150 an hour, and nearly £30,000 from one client so far.
  • £1k£1,000 a month, right now. About 6 hours a month. Around £165 an hour.
  • £10k£10,000 a month. A team of two, 2 to 3 days a week. £96 to £125 an hour before paying the second person. Less after.

The £10k retainer looked like the win. Per hour, it was the worst of the three, and it took most of my week. The £1k retainers are small, quick, and they've lasted years. That's why this kit doesn't just push you to charge more. It shows you what each hour actually pays, so you can choose: fewer, bigger clients, or more small ones that run without eating your life.

01 / The formulaFour questions, one number

Most creatives price from the bottom up: what do other people charge, what did I charge last time, what feels safe to say. That's how you end up at £500 shoots forever. Price comes from four questions, in this order.

  • 01Who is the client? A local café and a brand doing £5m a year can buy the same video. One of them can afford to pay you properly for it, and gets far more value from it. Who you sell to sets your ceiling more than anything else.
  • 02What's the brief, really? "Four videos a month" is the deliverable. The brief is the result they want: attention, leads, sales, a launch that lands. The closer you get to owning the result, the more the work is worth.
  • 03What does it cost to make? Your hours, your crew, your kit, your travel. Solo or team changes your price more than your camera ever will. This is your floor. Never go under it.
  • 04What do you want to make? Not what you think they'll pay. What the work needs to earn you for your business to be worth running. Decide it before the call, not during.

Questions three and four give you the floor. Questions one and two tell you how far above the floor you can go. That gap is where the money is, and it's where most people leave it on the table.

02 / The ladderWhat usually sits at each level
Level 01

£1k

Content support. Solo. A small business. A handful of edits a month, often from footage they send you. Great money if it takes you a day or two and you run several. A trap if it eats your week.

Level 02

£3k

Content partner. A shoot day a month, a set number of edits, a plan for what goes out. You're making decisions now, not just taking instructions.

Level 03

£5-10k

Growth partner. A brand with real budget. Strategy, production and reporting against the result they care about. Often an editor or second shooter under you.

Level 04

£15k+

Full service. You own the outcome across content, campaigns and launches, with a team doing the making. You're the expert they can't replace.

The climb

Every level is a step on the same ladder: more ownership of the result, a bigger client, and less of your own hours per pound. You don't have to climb to the top to do well. Plenty of people make a great living at level two with five or six clients and an editor. But if you want to climb, you don't do it by getting better at filming. You do it by changing who you sell to and how you sell.

02B / The honest partWhere the bigger money is

The bigger money is in owning the result.

Small retainers can pay well per hour. But the bigger money, generally, is in owning a result for a client: more customers, a launch that sells, a brand that grows. Owning a result takes a stack of skills, not one. Strategy, production, editing, copy, ads, reporting.

That stack used to mean a team. My £10k retainer needed two people and most of my week. It's never been easier to build the stack yourself: AI now does a lot of what that second person did, from first-cut edits to proposals to reporting. One creative, with the right agents, can own a result that used to need an agency.

That's the move: own more of the result, without adding more of your hours. Time in stays low. Money out goes up.

Build around constraint

Decide the life first: how many hours you'll work, what time is protected for your health and your family. Then build the business to fit inside it. A constraint like four-hour days makes the choice for you: you can't sell hours, so you have to own results and build leverage. Your number doesn't have to be seven figures. A £20k to £50k a month agency, built without being a slave to your clients, is a great business.

You win on expertise × leverage

Not as a service provider. Expertise is what lets you own the result and charge for it. Leverage is what stops it eating your week. One without the other caps you.

04 / Saying the numberWhere most people lose it

Diagnose before you prescribe

Don't send a price list. Ask about the business first: what they sell, what a new customer is worth, what's not working. A doctor doesn't hand you a menu. When the price comes after you've understood the problem, it lands as a prescription, not a cost.

Always three options

Give them A, B and C from the calculator. B is the one you want. C makes B look sensible. A is there so the answer is "which one", not "yes or no".

Say it, then stop

Say the number out loud and stop talking. No "but we can be flexible". No explaining. The silence feels long to you and normal to them. If the number makes you nervous, that's usually the sign it's right.

Monthly, not per project

Project work keeps you on the hamster wheel: win, deliver, start again from zero. Retainers give you a floor every month and a client relationship that grows. Fewer clients, paying more, every month.
